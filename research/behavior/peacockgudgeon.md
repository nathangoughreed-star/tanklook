# Peacock gudgeon (Tateurndina ocellicauda): behavior notes (R208)

Source: two ChatGPT answers Nathan gathered on 2026-11-28 (his open prompt, then C25's structured v1 prompt with percentages). This file is a digest. The originals are in Nathan's ChatGPT history and in the C25 chat transcript. No implementation until patterns generalize across species (R208).

## What both answers agree on (treat as solid)
- **Movement:** stop-and-go, not continuous cruising. Hover in place with small pectoral-fin movements, orient toward something, a short tail-driven move, decelerate, hover again. Short bursts when hunting or chasing. Body stays still while hovering (no swimming undulation).
- **Perching:** settles on sand, gravel, rock and wood. Resting on the bottom is normal, not illness. Separate pelvic fins and no suction disc, so it never clings to vertical surfaces like a true goby.
- **Where:** bottom third, just above the substrate, near plants and hardscape. Goes into the middle layer to explore and feed. Rarely open water, low-flow areas. No glass preference.
- **Caves:** comfortable fish are visible most of the time and are not cave-dwellers. Males adopt a preferred cave and keep returning to it after exploring, feeding or displaying. They stay in it for long stretches only while guarding eggs.
- **Own kind:** loose group, not a school. Fish stay near each other but move independently, with no synchronized heading. Males do lateral displays (side on, fins erect, colour up) and short chases, then return. Real fights are rare. Groups of 6-8 are typical, and pairs work too.
- **Breeding:** the male displays outside his cave and leads the female in. She lays on the cave ceiling, he drives her off, then guards and fans the eggs.
- **Feeding:** micropredator. Picks individual particles and strikes at moving food. Mostly low, sometimes rises. Slow and deliberate, so faster fish outcompete it.
- **Other species:** mostly ignores them (tetras, rasboras, corys, otos, snails, adult Amano shrimp). Disputes with dwarf cichlids and other gobies or gudgeons. Hunts shrimplets, cherry shrimp and fry. It reacts mainly when something comes near its occupied cave or competes for food. Startled, it retreats to plants, hardscape or a cave.
- **Cover effect:** more hiding places make it bolder and more exploratory. In a bare tank it moves less.
- **Stress vs. normal:** hovering, bottom resting and cave use are normal. Stress is persistent hiding, glass surfing, clamped fins, fast gills, refusing food, colour loss. New fish are shy for about two weeks.

## Unknown, according to the answers themselves
Distances from cover, territory radius, spacing between fish, speeds, pause lengths, night behavior, juvenile differences. Both answers say these aren't documented anywhere (the second marked every numeric field "Unknown").

## Prompt lessons
- The open prompt invented a percentage time budget (35 % hovering, 25 % perching ...) labelled as estimates. That's artificial precision, as Nathan expected.
- The v1 structured prompt honestly returned "Unknown" for every number, which confirms that numbers aren't in the training data.
- The most useful parts were the qualitative rating table (Low / Moderate / High per trait) in the open answer, and the animation lines: "should look comfortable doing nothing", "never suction-cling", "not a slow tetra".

## Candidate shared patterns (to test against the next species)
1. Movement style: continuous cruise / stop-go hover / perch-and-hop / glide on surfaces / burrow.
2. Home-site fidelity: a preferred spot (cave, leaf, patch), how far the fish ranges from it, and how it changes when breeding.
3. Surface contact: none / rests on the bottom / perches on hardscape / clings (suction) / buries.
4. Social cohesion: tight school / loose shoal / loose aggregation / pair / solitary.
5. Boldness scales with cover.
6. Startle response: dart to cover / school tightens / freeze / bury.
7. Feeding style: selective picker / sifter / grazer / surface snatcher / ambusher.
8. Breeding-state modifiers (territory up, cave occupation, guarding).
9. Trigger-based aggression (approach to its home site or food) vs. roaming aggression.
